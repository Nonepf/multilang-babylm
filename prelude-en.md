# Some Thoughts (and Ramblings)

I mainly worked on this project after finishing several demanding final exams near the end of my freshman year, during the short period before summer vacation.

During the second semester of my freshman year, after studying the basics of deep learning (CS188) and computer vision (CS231N), I began moving on to natural language processing (CS224N). While learning about artificial intelligence, I was also very interested in topics such as psychology and philosophy. When talking with Gemini about language and cognition, I became curious about whether there was somewhere people were actually studying these kinds of questions. Gemini recommended several websites. I looked through them one by one, and eventually my attention settled on BabyLM. The limited-data setting and the use of child-directed corpora immediately caught my interest.

After reading some papers from previous workshops, I also became eager to try doing something myself. Modifying the internals of models was beyond what I knew at the time, so I decided to work on something relatively simple and experimental: exploring whether, for a non-native speaker learning a second language, the relationship between the second language and their first language affects how difficult that language is to learn.

To be honest, I relied heavily on AI throughout the entire process—DeepSeek at first, and later ChatGPT. AI was involved in almost everything: discussing the experimental design, running experiments, writing code to analyze the data, and writing the paper. Sometimes it was almost like an advisor, helping me judge whether my experimental design was rigorous enough. Sometimes I would raise question after question about tiny details, and it would respond to them one by one. Sometimes it generated training code according to my requirements. Sometimes it extensively polished and rewrote the clumsy English in my paper. And sometimes this made me wonder: was this really work that I had done, or was I simply relying on AI?

I still remember that I used to dislike using AI to write code. I always felt that it reduced my understanding of the code itself and weakened the learning process. For course assignments, labs in self-study courses, and small projects, I basically wrote everything by hand. During the winter break of my freshman year, for example, I spent about a week doing a simple reimplementation of xv6 in Go ([xv6-in-go](https://github.com/Nonepf/xv6-in-go)). At the time, I printed out the entire official xv6 implementation and bound it into a booklet, worked out the relationships among the files, and then typed things out step by step, starting from a simple `Hello World` and gradually moving on to the various operating-system mechanisms. I did something similar with the CPU code afterward ([simple-cpu](https://github.com/Nonepf/Simple-CPU)). Following the architecture diagrams in *DDCA*, I implemented a single-cycle CPU, a multi-cycle CPU, and eventually a pipelined CPU in SystemVerilog, step by step. But after I entered deep learning, I seemed to start using AI more and more frequently. And the more I used AI, the more I realized just how inefficient it could be to write all the code myself. As a student, I am still trying to figure out where the boundary should be between using AI to write code and writing things by hand.

Back to this project itself. AI contributed a great deal, but I was also deeply involved in every step. I still remember repeatedly arguing with DeepSeek over the original research question and experimental design before we eventually arrived at an experimental setup that was manageable in scale and methodologically sound. I remember how difficult it was to quantify the “amount of information” across different languages, and how I repeatedly searched, asked questions, and weighed different possibilities before eventually settling on what seemed like a reasonably defensible solution. I remember renting GPUs on AutoDL myself, setting everything up, training and evaluating the models, and collecting the data. Several things went wrong along the way, and I had to keep tracing the causes and fixing them. And when it finally came time to write the paper, I wrote the first draft in Chinese, had DeepSeek translate and polish it, and then went through the English sentence by sentence myself, repeatedly reconsidering the wording.

When I submitted the paper, I honestly had no expectations about the result. The experiments were small in scale, the method itself was not novel, and there were still many difficult and unresolved questions surrounding the setup. More than anything, I treated it as a first attempt at research squeezed into the end of the semester, and as a chance to experience the entire process for myself. So when I received the conditional acceptance notification at the end of August, I was genuinely quite surprised. Of course, the reviewer did not evaluate the paper particularly highly, which was more or less what I had expected. But I was also pleasantly surprised by how detailed and constructive the reviewer’s suggestions for revision were.

The camera-ready version is now finished. Whether or not the paper is ultimately accepted, it has already been an interesting experience.
